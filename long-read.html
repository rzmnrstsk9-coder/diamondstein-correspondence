Prologue: Belarus and Latvia, 1770-1901

The Founding Generation — Zev Wulf

As far back as the Diamondstein family's own paper trail goes, it starts with Zev Wulf Dimantshtein, born around 1770 in Polotsk, in what is now Belarus, and dead before 1839 — no wife, no occupation, no title survives for him personally. (One other family chart gives David a different father and grandfather entirely: a father named Shlomo, who it says moved the family from Keidan, Lithuania to Korsovka, and — one generation further back — a rabbi named Tsvi-Hirsh HaLevy, married to a woman named Sirke, who headed a yeshiva in Keidan.) What does survive is a claim about the wider line: that they were Leviim and Chabad Chassidim, followers of the Hasidic movement then spreading through the Pale of Settlement. The same Hasidic identity turns up again a few generations later, when one of Zev Wulf's descendants married into a family whose patriarch was remembered as "one of the first Chabad Chassidim in England."

Worth Knowing
The Hasidic Movement
The 18th-century religious revival the family tree claims this family belonged to, and the Chabad school specifically.
Hasidism began in the mid-1700s in Podolia (in what's now Ukraine), founded by Israel ben Eliezer, known as the Baal Shem Tov -- a religious revival movement that stressed joyful, direct devotion and a charismatic rebbe's spiritual leadership over pure legal scholarship, at a time when Jewish religious life in Eastern Europe was dominated by rabbinic academies. It spread quickly through the Pale of Settlement over the following decades, splitting into distinct courts and dynasties, each following its own rebbe. Chabad -- the specific branch this family's own tree names -- was founded a generation later by Rabbi Shneur Zalman of Liadi, and took root especially in the Belarus/Lithuania/Latvia corner of the Pale this family is documented in, Polotsk included. None of this is confirmed by any letter or document specific to this family -- it comes from a claim in one family-tree chart -- but it's the real, well-documented religious landscape a Polotsk family of this era would have belonged to if the chart's claim holds.
Worth Knowing
The Pale of Settlement
The legal confinement and tightening restrictions that pushed an entire generation of shtetl families to leave.
Korsovka sat inside the Pale of Settlement, the region of the Russian Empire where Jewish residence was legally confined from 1791 onward, roughly the territory of modern Belarus, Lithuania, Latvia, Moldova, and parts of Ukraine and Poland. Villages like it were built around a synagogue, a marketplace, and a mix of trades and small commerce. Through the 1880s and 90s the Empire tightened the legal noose further: the May Laws of 1882 restricted new Jewish settlement even within the Pale itself, quotas capped Jewish enrollment in schools and universities, and conscription pressure on young Jewish men intensified. None of this is documented for this specific family, there's no letter describing it, but it's the ordinary, well-attested backdrop against which an entire generation of shtetl families, this one included, made the same calculation at roughly the same time: stay, or go.

Zev Wulf's Son Eliyahu

Zev Wulf's son Eliyahu, born around 1800, also in Polotsk, died sometime before 1885. Nothing else about his own life survives — no wife, no occupation.

Eliyahu's Son David and Wife Keila-Tsirel, Merchants, Farmers, Inn Keepers

Eliyahu's son David Dimantshtein married Keila-Tsirel, and together they built something real: an 1889 census recorded David as a merchant, and the family was wealthy — running an inn and working farmland on the outskirts of a small Vitebsk Governorate shtetl. They had already moved once, from Polotsk to Rezhitza; in 1872, David moved them again, on to Korsovka — the shtetl Sam himself would later call home.

Eleven Children: Korsovka, Riga, London, New York

David and Keila-Tsirel had eleven children — a twelfth is named by one family account and left off another, so even the record itself isn't sure he belongs on the list — and between them this next generation shows a family mostly staying put, but not entirely. Several stayed within the Baltic region itself — farming and trading in Korsovka and Riga. A cluster reached London: some became furriers and drapers — while a sister among them, Rivka, became a philanthropist, a founder of the Stepney Jewish Hospital. Another sister, Zissa, married and started a family nearly wiped out by the Holocaust in the generations that followed. One sibling, Zalman — known as Solomon — crossed an ocean for good, to New York. The far longer journeys — to Montreal, to Palestine, and further still — belonged to the next generation, David and Keila-Tsirel's grandchildren, not to their own eleven children.

Zissa's Family in the Holocaust

One of David Dimantshtein's daughters, Zissa and her family paid the heaviest price. She had married Peisakh Gordin; their son, Yaakov-Zev-Wolf Gordin, and his wife, Batia Nisse, were both killed in Korsovka in 1941. Of their six children, three — Rassia, Zalman, and Mikhail — were killed that same year in Korsovka; the other three — Gita, Liuba, and Pesakh-Eliyahu — survived and reached what would become Israel. Two more of Zissa's sons, Mendel and Dan Gordin, also died in 1941, in Berzpils, along with most of their own children and grandchildren — though Mendel's son Aba Gordin survived, and after the war organized religious services for what remained of Korsovka's Jewish community.

Zissa's line was not the only one this generation lost. Her brother Zev-Wolf had stayed in Korsovka too, farming near the town's Jewish cemetery and Naudas Kalns — the site of the 1941 massacre itself. Of his own children, at least three were killed there and in Riga that same year: his son Avraham, Avraham's wife Liza, and their daughter Marita, killed together in Korsovka; and two of Avraham's nieces, Zhenia and Gittel, killed in a roundup of women in Riga. A grandson, Zev-Wolf — known in the family as "Volinka" — was killed in Riga the same year, a university student. The wider family's war losses reached further still, in different ways: one grandson died at the front serving in the Soviet army in 1944; two more were killed serving in the Soviet and Latvian armies; a son, Shmuel, fled across the Soviet border in 1941 and perished in Siberian exile; and another son, Eliyahu, died of illness in exile in Kokand, Uzbekistan, in 1942.

Reuven, Arrested and Exiled to Siberia

Dora's father Reuven's own story took a darker turn before the family reached England. Family memory says he was arrested and sent to Siberia, and his wife Hinde followed him there — though what he was actually charged with isn't agreed on even within the family. One account says he was accused of horse theft, a charge Dora herself always maintained was invented, covering what was really a political exile. Another says he was accused of setting fire to crops. Nothing dates the episode, and the family's own memory doesn't settle which charge, if either, was real.

Reuven and Getzel's Children: Sam and Dora

Dora's uncle, Getzel, was the father of Sam, making Sam and Dora first cousins, raised in the same small-town world their grandparents had built, in the same faith their great-great-grandfather's line was remembered for a century before — and they would grow up to marry each other, and become the two people this entire story is actually about.

Worth Knowing
Marriage Between Cousins
Why Sam and Dora marrying as first cousins raised no eyebrows at the time.
Sam and Dora were first cousins, their fathers, Getzel and Reuven, were brothers. That's not an aside; it's a real structural fact of how this correspondence exists at all: cousin marriage was a common, unremarkable, religiously permitted practice in small, close-knit shtetl communities like Korsovka, often reinforcing family and property ties across a scattering diaspora rather than raising any concern. Sam and Dora were plausibly raised knowing each other from early childhood, and by the time the earliest surviving letters begin, courtship between them reads as an entirely ordinary, expected next step, not a complication the letters ever feel a need to explain.

Sam and Dora Reach England

Reuven reached London in 1897, the last of his own siblings to leave Korsovka — his son Yehuda-Leib, then seventeen, came with him, but later moved to Palestine, where he was killed in the 1929 Palestine riots. Dora herself followed a few years later, arriving in England around 1903 or 1904, still a girl of about fourteen. One granddaughter's testimony names a relative, "Uncle Bernard," as the one who brought Dora and her family back from Siberia to England — though which Bernard isn't settled either; the only Bernard identified in the corpus so far belongs to a different branch of the family entirely, so the name may not be reliable. Getzel never left Korsovka at all, and stayed there the rest of his life — whether he was even still alive to see his son leave for Montreal in 1901 isn't known; no source gives a death date for him that actually holds up. Sam may have made this journey in two steps rather than one. A possible 1901 English census record has him in England before that — which would mean he crossed to Montreal from there, not straight from Korsovka as his own dated letter home, postmarked Montreal, June 1901, had previously been assumed to mean. Either way, he didn't settle in London for good until 1908 — arriving in the same city his cousin Dora already called home. A family photo shows Sam, Dora, and Sam's own mother together, Sam plainly too old in it to still have been in Korsovka — meaning she, at least, made it to England too, at some point after her son did.

Chapter One, The Courtship · circa 1900–1911

Sam Leaves Korsovka

In 1901, Sam, around twenty years old (the corpus's birth-year estimates for him range from 1880 to 1885 and don't yet agree), left Korsovka alone for Montreal, overland to a Channel port, then steerage across the Atlantic on one of the emigrant lines running from Europe to Canada. It is this single decision that starts the correspondence. From this point on, whatever happens between Sam and Dora happens on paper, across an ocean, for the next decade.

Worth Knowing
How Emigration Worked
The well-documented transit system Sam's 1901 crossing almost certainly rode on.
Sam's home region fed one of the best-documented transmigration systems of the era: the Wilson Line of Hull. By 1900, 45,548 Jewish transmigrants passed through the port of Hull alone in a single year, up from just 500 to 1,000 a year in the mid-1850s. From Hull, most travelled by a dedicated boat train straight through to Liverpool, then across the Atlantic by steamer, in effect a sealed corridor, where many people in Sam's exact position never really “entered” England at all in the sense of stopping to live there. Sam is first documented in Montreal on 11 June 1901, a plausible age and route for this exact system, though no ship manifest for his own crossing survives to confirm it.
Worth Knowing
Letters, Before Telephones
Why fast, reliable post, not telephones, made these letters feel more like texting than formal correspondence.
Home telephones were genuinely rare into the 1940s, the whole archive contains exactly one phone mention, and even that one is a borrowed line at an aunt's house. What made letters work instead was the post itself: early-1900s London had four deliveries a day, and the transatlantic Allan Line ran weekly, crossing Liverpool to Montreal in about five days. A letter posted in Montreal could get a reply within two to three weeks; a London letter could get one the same evening. Fast enough that these read less like slow, formal correspondence and more like today's back-and-forth texting, daily during the Montreal courtship, weekly clockwork during the seaside years.

The Wilson Steamship Line

Sam's home region used one of the best-documented transmigration systems of the era: the Wilson Steamship Line of Hull. By 1900, 45,548 Jewish transmigrants passed through the port of Hull alone in a single year, up from just 500 to 1,000 a year in the mid-1850s, a massive, organized operation rather than an improvised route. From Hull, most travelled by a dedicated boat train, the North Eastern Railway, straight through to Liverpool via Leeds, Huddersfield, and Stalybridge; in 1900 alone, over 31,000 transmigrants moved this way. It was, in effect, a sealed corridor, many people in Sam's exact position never really stayed in England at all in the sense of stopping to live there, just boarded a designated train at Hull and rode it to the next ship. Sam is first documented in Montreal on 11 June 1901, in his early twenties, an ordinary age for solo emigration, and a plausible one to have followed this exact route, though no ship manifest or travel record for his own crossing survives to confirm it. Whether he stopped meaningfully in London along the way, or passed straight through the corridor to Montreal, isn't known either way.

Sam Tries to Make a Life in Canada

Montreal did not go smoothly. Sam crossed back and forth more than once across this decade, cycling through jobs that didn't last, and in 1907 the Panic of that year wiped out whatever he'd managed to build. "When you have no money," he wrote to uncle Reuven, "there is nothing at all you can do."

Worth Knowing
The Panic of 1907
Sam arrived in Montreal just as a real financial panic gutted the casual-labor work he needed.
Sam landed in Montreal in 1901 chasing casual and fur-trade work, and arrived just in time for one of the worst stretches to be doing it. His own wages started around 25 cents a day, roughly an eighth of what U.S. workers were already demanding as a bare subsistence minimum in 1900. Then came the Panic of 1907, a real transatlantic financial crisis that threw Montreal's economy into its own contraction, Sam's own letters describe men thrown out of work “by the hundreds,” himself included. It's a big part of why, after nearly a decade of going back and forth, he finally gave up on Montreal for good in 1908.

The earliest letter in the whole archive, dated 11 June 1901, spells out exactly this kind of small, practical deception immigrants of the period sometimes had to make just to keep working. Turned away for being a foreign Jew, Sam told the hiring office he was an Austrian Christian instead, and they believed him.

Just how bad Sam's own Montreal wages actually were is worth putting real numbers to, rather than taking his word for it. In one letter he reports earning about a dollar for four days' work at first, 25 cents a day, improving later to "three or three and a half dollars" in a good week. For comparison: in 1900, New Orleans workers were already petitioning for a "living wage" floor of 25 cents an hour, eight hours a day, two dollars a day, proposed as bare subsistence, not comfort. Sam's initial rate was roughly one-eighth of what workers elsewhere were demanding as a subsistence minimum, two years before he wrote that letter. Even his improved rate doesn't hold up well against the era: American wage-earning men averaged $11.16 a week in 1905, and Montreal's own municipal street laborers were raised to $2.10 a day in 1912, later than Sam's letter, and different work, but the closest same-city anchor available. Generously assuming his "three to three-and-a-half dollars" covered a full six-day week, that puts him at roughly a quarter to a third of a comparable Montreal laborer's wage just a few years on, and under a third of the 1905 U.S. average. His own words in the same letter, "life here is very dry", read like plain description against these numbers, not homesick exaggeration.

Montreal wasn't the only alternative to giving up and sailing home. Sam turns over at least three others in these letters, none developed as far as the land-grant scheme. Winnipeg and Minnesota each surface only once, and only in passing. In one undated letter he declares he has “come to a decision” about a possible move to Winnipeg; in another, already doing itinerant tailoring work after fleeing what he calls “wretched Canada,” he admits he's “still not at all sure” he'll stay in Minnesota. Chicago pulled harder and longer, it surfaces in five separate letters from this stretch, more than any option besides the homestead scheme. Once I receive the two pounds from you, I'll pay off what I owe and set off, perhaps for Chicago, he writes in one, jobless again; in another, an acquaintance's report that “in Chicago it is better than in Montreal” seems to have stuck with him. When he does go, in a letter that reads as much like a self-defense as a plan, he insists it isn't restlessness; “I want to do it to make a real life for myself, not to go chasing after fortunes for now in these golden lands.” Most of these letters carry no date of their own, assigned only an approximate 1907–1909 window from internal clues, and several run through low-confidence transcription spans; what the record shows clearly is Sam turning four different ways over, not committing to any of them, before Montreal fails him for the second time and London becomes the answer instead.

Of the options Sam was weighing as a way out of Montreal, the one he put real thought into was a Canadian government land grant, worth being precise about which one, since the phrase "homestead grant" usually means something specific that doesn't quite match what he describes. The famous version, the Dominion Lands Act homestead, was free: 160 acres for a ten-dollar administration fee, in exchange for "proving up" over three years, building a permanent dwelling and hitting staged cultivation targets. What Sam describes instead requires real capital up front (on the order of a thousand dollars, by his own account), a much smaller ten-acre parcel, and a house already provided, terms that line up far better with a different, lesser-known scheme: the "Ready-Made Farms" the Canadian Pacific Railway ran in Alberta and Saskatchewan in roughly this period, where the company built the house and barn, dug the well, and ploughed the land before a settler ever arrived, in exchange for real cash on landing. Neither match is exact, some of Sam's own figures are low-confidence machine-transcription guesses, but the shape of what he's actually weighing is a paid, furnished start on someone else's already-broken land, not the free frontier homestead the phrase might suggest to a modern reader.

By 1908, Sam gave up on Canada for good and went to London instead. It's the decision that actually determines the rest of the story, made only after Montreal had already failed him, not as a first choice but as what was left.

Sam Gives Up on Canada and Returns to England

The resolution comes in two letters that read like a before-and-after. In July 1908 he is still conditional, as soon as he gets work, he'll send for Dora. A month later, on the second of August, the conditional becomes a decision: work is secured, Montreal life is "very dry," and he'll do far better in London. Some of that apparent whiplash may not be whiplash at all: the Allan Line's Liverpool–Montreal mail service ran weekly, every Thursday, with the crossing itself taking five to five and a half days on its newest turbine steamers, genuinely fast for the era, when most transatlantic crossings still ran seven to ten days. Add the wait for the next Thursday sailing on either end, and a single letter's real transit time could run anywhere from about six days, if timed well, to twelve or thirteen, if it just missed one. Two letters posted a week or so apart, each responding to something the other hadn't seen yet, could easily read like a sudden reversal without actually being one. Dora sends three pounds toward Sam's return ticket that August.

Sam and Dora Marry

Sometime around February 1911, Sam and Dora married, cousins who'd courted almost entirely by letter across an ocean for a decade. No photograph of the day survives. The marriage came with real money attached: Reuven's own signed tenaim obligated him to a fifty-pound dowry, quite possibly the first real capital Sam ever had — whether it was the actual seed money for the fur business he was running five years later isn't something the record can confirm.

Underneath all of this sits a document that survives in far better shape than any letter: the January 1908 tenaim, the traditional pre-wedding engagement contract, which Reuven and Sam's side both signed. It confirms in writing what the family tree only implies, Reuven ben David HaLevi obligating himself to give Sam a fifty-pound dowry "before the wedding," with a guarantor standing behind the promise, and a specific penalty built into the document itself: whichever side failed to keep its conditions would forfeit half a dowry. The same contract's wedding-expenses clause spells out what that money was for: "Rabbi, Cantor, Shamash, and band" -- klezmer is this project's own gloss for the period wedding band the document just calls "band" -- a real wedding, budgeted for, in a document dated three years before Sam and Dora actually married in February 1911.

Worth Knowing
Klezmer: The Band the Tenaim Already Budgeted For
The itinerant wedding-band tradition named as a real line item in Sam and Dora's own 1908 marriage contract.
“Klezmer” comes from the Yiddish klei-zemer, literally “instruments of song”, the itinerant instrumental tradition Ashkenazi Jewish musicians played at weddings and other celebrations across Central and Eastern Europe from at least the 16th century onward. A typical band mixed clarinet, violin, and later accordion, often alongside a badkhn, a hired wedding bard and master of ceremonies who improvised rhymes for the occasion. This isn't general background dressed up as this family's story: the January 1908 tenaim itself line-items “Rabbi, Cantor, Shamash, and klezmer band” as a real wedding expense, budgeted three years before Sam and Dora actually married. The site's own ambient background music is a klezmer-inspired piece composed for this project, AI-generated, not a recording of anyone's actual wedding band.

Chapter Two, The War Years · 1914–1918

Sam and Dora Have Children and He's Running His Fur Business

By September 1916, Sam and Dora are married with children, running S. Diamondstein & Co., Fur Dealers and Job Buyers, out of Fashion Street and Fournier Street in Brick Lane — the earliest address of Sam's own business this archive can attest to — home at 34 Alvington Crescent, Dalston. It's a short chapter by letter count, but a real hinge: the courtship-at-a-distance voice of the last chapter gives way here to the provider-from-a-distance voice that defines the next decade, one early letter on that same stationery shows exactly what it sounds like: money enclosed, questions about how the children are growing, and money sent on to Dora's sister too.

Worth Knowing
A Name That Kept Changing
How one family surname split into two different endings, in two different countries.
“Dimantshtein”, Yiddish for “diamond-stone”, is how the family tree itself spells the name; “Diamondstein” is the standing English form for decades, on Sam's 1916 business letterhead and still in use on a 1942 War Damage notice. That same week, a piano-shop invoice to the same household already reads “Mrs. Diamond”, the shortened form arriving alongside the fuller name. Sam himself carries it one step further later in life, signing as “S. DYMOND, Fur and Skin Merchant.” Meanwhile Dora's brother Yehuda-Leib's own son, emigrating to Palestine instead of England, adopted the surname “Yahalomi”, Hebrew for diamond, a direct translation rather than a softened spelling. Same root word, two branches, two different countries, two entirely different assimilations.
Glossary
Job Buyer
Part of Sam's own business title, a dealer who buys up job lots (bulk surplus or off-quality stock) rather than made-to-order goods.
Worth Knowing
The Anglo-Jewish East End
The real geography behind every address in these letters, and the family's own rise through it.
The addresses in these letters trace a real, well-documented migration pattern. Sam's business ran out of Fashion Street and Fournier Street in Brick Lane, with the family living at 34 Alvington Crescent, Dalston. Margate, Ramsgate, Westcliff, and Bournemouth, the seaside resorts Dora and the girls returned to summer after summer, had become close to a specifically Anglo-Jewish seaside circuit by this period.

How the Mail System Worked When Phones Weren't Common

It's worth pausing here on how this whole correspondence was even possible, because the answer isn't obvious from a modern vantage point. Home telephones were genuinely rare and expensive through this entire period, ownership was mostly confined to businesses and the wealthy, and a basic public call-box cost thirteen pounds to install as late as 1925. Private domestic telephones didn't become commonplace in ordinary households until well after the Second World War. Against that backdrop, the 140-plus-letter archive contains only a small handful of personal telephone mentions across more than four decades: in August 1924, Sam writes that he'll phone Dora “today from auntie's”, not from his own home, but by walking to a relative's house to use hers; a near-identical line in a 1921 letter (its exact wording contested between transcription passes); a 1926 letter from Paris asking a colleague to phone a third party on his behalf; and an undated Berlin-trip fragment asking that his telephone messages be relayed to him daily while away. Business letterheads from the same years do print telephone numbers — the business itself was reachable by phone — but every personal mention in Sam's own hand describes phoning from, or through, somewhere other than his own home. That pattern, not a bare count, is the real evidence that a home telephone stayed out of reach for this family well into the 1920s, even for a comparatively comfortable London business household.

What made letters work instead was a postal system fast enough to make the absence of a phone a non-issue. Early-1900s London had four postal deliveries a day, the last as late as 9:30pm, routine enough that a letter posted in the morning could get a reply the same evening. That infrastructure explains why the correspondence reads the way it does: near-daily when Sam was away buying fur, weekly clockwork during the seaside years still to come, without any sense of a long wait for a reply. The corpus even shows a deliberate two-tier system underneath that rhythm, a quick, cheap postcard for a fast update, a proper letter reserved for anything that needed more room, with at least one letter explicitly correcting a family member's assumption that a postcard and a letter were the same thing.

Sam Watches a Zeppelin Being Shot Down

Just once, an ordinary letter home runs straight into history. Sam mentions that he and his friend John watched a German Zeppelin shot down over Cuffley, Hertfordshire, “it was a fine sight”, then returns to asking after the kiddies at the seaside, in the same letter.

Worth Knowing
The Cuffley Zeppelin
The real WWI event Sam watched fall from a restaurant with his friend John.
The airship Sam and John watched fall was SL11, popularly called “the Cuffley Zeppelin,” though it was actually a Schütte-Lanz design, not a true Zeppelin-built craft. It came down over Cuffley, Hertfordshire, on the night of 2–3 September 1916, during the largest German airship raid on Britain to that point. Royal Flying Corps pilot Lieutenant William Leefe Robinson intercepted it and shot it down using new incendiary ammunition designed to ignite the airship's hydrogen, the first German airship destroyed over mainland Britain. It burned as it fell, visible across London, with crowds reportedly watching and cheering; all 16 crew died. Robinson was awarded the Victoria Cross almost immediately. Sam's letter is dated the very next morning, an independently-dated public event lining up almost to the hour with his own account.

Chapter Three, The Travelling Years · 1919–1926

The Family Retreats to the Seaside While Sam Works in London and Takes Business Trips to the Continent

Dora and the girls summer at the English seaside for weeks at a stretch, Margate, Ramsgate, Westcliff, Bournemouth — a health retreat from London's air, by family account — while Sam works London and buys fur on the Continent. Every summer, Dora and the girls went to the coast for a stretch. And on his buying trips Sam was away a few days at a time, and wrote home while he was gone.

Worth Knowing
“Change of Air”
The real Edwardian medical convention behind sending Dora and the girls to the coast every summer.
“Change of air” was a real medical convention of the Edwardian period: doctors routinely prescribed extended coastal or country stays for children and convalescents as general health practice, independent of any specific illness — the ordinary backdrop this family's own seaside stays fit into.
Family Testimony
The seaside stays were a health retreat from London's smog
Not stated in any letter — this is Paul's own family knowledge, not something any letter in the corpus says.
The seaside stays themselves are letter-attested: the destinations, the dates, the tone all come from the correspondence. Why the family went is not — no letter gives a reason. That the trips were meant as a health retreat from London's air comes from Paul's own family knowledge. It's plausible: "change of air" was a real, well-documented Edwardian medical convention (doctors routinely prescribing extended coastal or country stays as general health practice). But that convention being real doesn't make it documented for this specific family — it makes the family's own account of why they went a reasonable one, not a proven one.

Through these years, Sam's letters settle into a steady rhythm: a cheque folded into an envelope, then the same handful of questions about the children, the weather, whether they're behaving, asked and answered, week after week, for the better part of a decade.

The business, meanwhile, becomes genuinely wealthy. By the 1920s Sam is buying pelts by the thousand on regular trips to Paris and Leipzig, ninety stone martens and three thousand dyed moles in a single afternoon, cheap enough to sell at a steep markup back home. The boy who once earned twenty-five cents a day in Montreal was, twenty years on, moving more money in an afternoon than most of Montreal ever paid him in a year. A September 1921 trip has him writing from the Grand Hôtel du Pavillon in Paris, business apparently going well enough to allow an afternoon off: "Paris is a fine place; I have been to a Turkish bath this afternoon."

Worth Knowing
The Fur Trade, London to the Continent
London was the shopfront, the real buying happened on regular trips to Paris, Leipzig, and Berlin.
By 1916 Sam was running S. Diamondstein & Co., Fur Dealers and Job Buyers, out of Fashion Street in London's Brick Lane, but London was the storefront, not the source. The actual buying happened on regular trips to Paris, Leipzig, and Berlin, where he'd purchase pelts by the thousand, stone martens, dyed moles, cheap enough to sell at a steep markup back home; one single day's purchase of three thousand moles (the ninety martens carry no price in the letter, and aren't part of this estimate), sold on at roughly three times what he'd paid for them — a margin that holds regardless of pricing units, but the letter doesn't say whether "42 francs" was priced per skin or per dozen, and that unresolved reading swings the total estimate roughly twelvefold. On the more conservative per-dozen reading, it works out to something like sixty thousand Canadian dollars of gross profit today; on the per-skin reading, into the hundreds of thousands. That's why so many letters from the 1919–1926 “Travelling Years” are written on Continental hotel stationery.

What the Letters Don't Say · the silences + after

Not every part of this story is in what the letters say. Some of the most telling moments here are in what they don't say.

Why No Letters in This Archive From Dora

Of the 140-plus letters in this archive, Sam is the confirmed author of the great majority. Not one is written by Dora herself. Her side of a decade of daily correspondence, and years after that, simply don't survive, if it was ever kept at all.

Family testimony offers a real explanation, not just an absence. Dora left Latvia for England at fourteen and went straight to work rather than to school, and by her granddaughter Hilary's account, her English reading and writing stayed limited for most of her adult life, she didn't take it up seriously until her eighties, decades after this correspondence, taking lessons alongside the family's au pairs so she could read the newspaper aloud to Sam once his own sight failed. Sam, not Dora, wrote the family's birthday cards. Hilary's own guess is that when a reply did go out in Dora's name, one of the children likely wrote it for her.

The Weimar Years, in the Background

Fourteen items in this archive reference Leipzig, Berlin, or Hanover. The eight that carry a date fall between 1922 and 1925 — the years of the Weimar Republic's currency collapse, when hyperinflation peaked and effectively ended by November 1923 — so this framing fits the letters from 1922–23 more precisely than the ones from 1924–25, written after the mark had been stabilized by the Rentenmark. Sam was doing business through all of it, and none of it appears in his letters home, which stay focused on hotels, prices, and missing his family. What this archive cannot tell you is what came next. It holds nothing at all between 1926 and 1941 — the Nazi Party's rise to power in January 1933 falls entirely inside that gap. The silence is the archive's, not the family's: these are the letters that happened to be kept, and their ending is not an ending to anything except the record.

Afterword

The Name That Kept Changing

One last thread, easy to miss because it plays out across decades rather than inside any single letter: the family's own name never stops quietly changing shape. “Dimantshtein”, Yiddish for “diamond-stone”, is how the family tree itself spells the name; “Diamondstein” was the standard English-language form for decades on Sam's 1916 business letterhead and still in use as late as the 1942 War Damage notice, addressed to “H. Diamondstein, Esq.” A little over two weeks later, at minimum — a piano-shop invoice to the same household already reads “Mrs. Diamond”, the shortened, anglicized form arriving alongside the fuller name, not yet replacing it. Sam himself carries it one step further still, later in life: a business letterhead reading “S. DYMOND, Fur and Skin Merchant,” signed personally to a small child in words that read, as best the faded original allows, as “Great Grandpa and Great Granma Dymond” — marked low-confidence in the transcription, not a certain reading. That child was Paul, this project's own author, and Sam and Dora's great-grandson.

Not every branch of the family anglicized the sound of the name the same way, one branch translated its meaning instead. Yehuda-Leib, Dora's brother, emigrated to Jerusalem and died there in 1929, wounded during the Arab attacks on the Diskin Orphanage that were part of that year's Palestine riots. His son Avraham later adopted the surname “Yahalomi”, Hebrew for diamond, a direct translation of the same root rather than a softened English spelling of it. Two branches of one family, a generation apart, each solving the same problem, a foreign-sounding surname, in a new country, completely differently, depending on which country each one actually landed in: England sanded the sound down (Diamondstein to Diamond to Dymond), while Palestine translated the meaning instead. Same root word, two different assimilations, shaped entirely by geography.

The Journey of the Letters

None of this, not one letter, not one photograph, not one name change, would be sitting in front of a reader today by anything like a straight line. Robin, Gladys's own son, took a folder of these letters sometime in the 1960s, while living for a time with Sam and Dora themselves. In the 1990s he sent them on to Richard, Paul's brother, who was researching the family tree at the time and had asked relatives for whatever they had. Richard died in 1999. Paul found the letters years later, still sitting in his late brother's own files, and that, a three-generation chain — one relative's keeping, a second relative's unfinished genealogy project, and a third relative's own storage, rediscovered years after inheriting it, is the entire reason any of this survived to become a project at all. The letters were never lost so much as quietly handed forward — twice actively, and then, after a gap of years following Richard's death, rediscovered rather than passed on — by people who each had their own separate reason for holding onto them a little longer.
